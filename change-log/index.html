Change log

  1. 5 October 2026

    Shannon’s guessing game

    A reading exhibit rather than a machine: you guess a passage one letter at a time and the page records nothing but how many attempts each letter took. In 1951 Claude Shannon ran exactly this on his wife Mary to measure the entropy of English, having no machine to ask. The ranks are enough on their own — given the same guesser you can rebuild the text from them, so a thing cannot carry more information than the numbers it is recoverable from. He landed on roughly 0.6 to 1.3 bits per letter against the 4.75 you would need if all 27 symbols were equally likely, and that gap is the reason text compresses at all.

    The live readout is the entropy of your own guess-rank distribution, labelled as the upper bound it actually is. Three passages: Shannon’s own test sentence from the paper, and the openings of Moby-Dick and Pride and Prejudice. Expect your number to fall the further in you get, which is the whole bet behind next-token prediction.

    The maths was checked against hand-computed values: a perfect guesser reads 0.00 bits, and a two-thirds/one-third split of first- and second-try guesses reads 0.92, matching H = 0.9183. Guessing goes through a real text input rather than a key listener, so a phone keyboard works and pressing space never scrolls the page.

  2. 5 October 2026

    The consoles are shareable now

    Dial in a rhythm on the drum machine or a configuration on the slime mould and the URL updates to match; there is a Copy link button on both. Reload or send it to someone and the settings come back exactly — a 141 bpm E(4,11) kick is now a thing you can hand to a person rather than describe. Until today, reloading threw all of it away.

    State lives in the query string, deliberately not the hash: the section rail already owns the hash for its anchors, and clicking a rail link would have wiped any parameters stored there. Writes go through history.replaceState, throttled, so dragging a slider doesn’t push a hundred entries onto the back button. Everything read back out of a URL is treated as hostile — it is whatever somebody pasted — so each exhibit clamps its own values and falls back to defaults on anything malformed.

    A modification run rather than a new exhibit: it touched both euclid.js and slime.js plus a new shared helper. Tested against deliberately broken URLs (?r=garbage, out-of-range numbers, wrong field counts) — all clamp or fall back with nothing thrown.

  3. 5 October 2026

    Spacewar! (1962), playable

    A working Spacewar! on the daily build — the first video game that spread. Steve Russell wrote it at MIT in 1962 for a PDP‑1 with nine kilobytes of core and a round point-plotting CRT. Nobody sold it; people copied it onto paper tape by hand and carried it to the next PDP‑1 until DEC gave up and shipped it as the machine’s power-on diagnostic. First to seven, against an opponent that will dodge the star to save itself before it takes a shot at you.

    The star has real inverse-square gravity and it is the whole game: both ships open in a true circular orbit (v = √(GM/r), verified holding a 180px radius), and flying straight at someone loses to flying an arc. Two details are faithful rather than fixed: hyperspace gets you out of anything with about a one-in-six chance of not arriving, and torpedoes ignore the star’s gravity — true of Russell’s version, never corrected, because it played better.

    The keyboard is captured only while the field has focus, so arrow keys still scroll the page everywhere else and Tab always leaves — both verified, since a game that eats your arrow keys on a scrolling page is a trap.

  4. 5 October 2026

    Horologium — five clocks that disagree

    A new exhibit on the daily build, all of it running live off your own system clock: Roman seasonal hours, French Revolutionary decimal time, Swatch .beats, local mean solar time at Jezero crater on Mars, and the Unix epoch counting down to its own 2038 overflow. No network, no timezone database — arithmetic on Date.now().

    I picked it for the Roman one. An hour was a twelfth of daylight, so it stretched in summer and shrank in winter — today in Seattle a night hour runs about 63 minutes — and a clock that ticked evenly would have been the broken one. Sun times use the NOAA solar position algorithm; Mars follows Allison & McEwen (2000), the formulation JPL uses to put rover teams on Mars time, where they drift forty minutes later every day through their own nights.

    Deliberately the quietest thing on the page — a table, after a display case, a drum console and a glowing petri dish. The only thing that moves is the digits.

  7. 5 October 2026

    The drum machine now feeds the slime mould

    Two sections that had nothing to do with each other are wired together. Tick Feed from Euclid on the Physarum console and every onset the drum machine plays drops attractant onto the mould’s field, at the position that beat occupies on Euclid’s own rings — the voice picks the radius, the step picks the angle. The rhythm gets drawn onto the field and the network grows along it, so a tresillo and a 13-step drift build visibly different organisms.

    It is deliberately one-way and loosely coupled: euclid.js broadcasts a daily:beat event on document and knows nothing about who listens; slime.js listens and never reaches into Euclid. Delete either section and the other carries on. The event fires on the visual drain rather than the audio scheduler, so a listener lands on the beat you actually hear rather than up to 120ms ahead of it.

    Fourth of five changes made in one sitting, and the one the old additive-only rule made flatly impossible — it required editing two previously-committed scripts. Best seen by pausing the mould, hitting Reseed, then ticking the box: the rings draw themselves onto the empty field.

  8. 5 October 2026

    A section rail and a scroll-progress bar

    The daily build is six sections and several thousand pixels deep, with no way to get around it but scrolling from the top. There is now a dot rail down the right edge — one dot per section, label unfolding on hover or focus, current section marked — and a two-pixel progress bar across the very top.

    The progress bar is pure CSS: animation-timeline: scroll() makes the scroll position the playhead, so there is no scroll listener on the page at all. The rail uses an IntersectionObserver that tracks the largest visible section rather than the first to cross a line — with a 100svh hero, a short About and some very tall consoles, “first to cross” flickers between neighbours at every boundary. The rail hides below 1040px, where it would sit on top of the content.

    Third of five changes made in one sitting. Structural rather than decorative — the page had outgrown having no navigation.

  9. 5 October 2026

    One panel system instead of three

    The Shelf’s display case, Euclid’s drum console and Physarum’s field had each grown their own copy of the same dark panel — same gradient, same inset rim along the top edge, same drop shadow, same pill button with a glowing status dot, same ghost button, same select, same focus ring — written out three times under three different prefixes. That is now one file, css/panel.css, and each section keeps its identity by setting four custom properties and nothing else.

    182 lines were deleted from euclid.css, slime.css and shelf.css, and the markup was rewired to the shared .panel-* classes. This is precisely the “clean up or consolidate earlier days’ work” the old rule forbade by name; with the rule lifted it was the most obviously overdue thing on the page. Nothing looks different — each panel resolves to the exact gradient and accent it had before, which is the point of a refactor.

    Second of five changes made in one sitting. Verified afterwards that all six Shelf devices, Euclid’s four rings and patterns, and the Physarum canvas still work, and that each section’s status dot still lights in its own colour.

  10. 5 October 2026

    Dark mode — and the end of the additive-only rule

    The daily build now has a real theme system: a three-state toggle in the banner (Auto → Light → Dark), remembered across visits, defaulting to whatever your OS asks for and following it live if you change it mid-visit. An inline script in the <head> resolves the stored choice before first paint so there is no flash of the wrong theme.

    This changed existing files, which until today was forbidden. Ruslan lifted the additive-only rule, so daily/css/intro.css was edited in place: the hero’s hardcoded #fff background and #111 text now read from the theme variables, and its white shine sweep routes through a variable the dark theme dims. The experimental-build banner was rewritten to hold the toggle. Everything else turned over for free, because the sections were already written against the custom properties in style.css.

    First of five changes made in one sitting to see what the page does under a looser rule. The scope rule is untouched — the real homepage remains off limits, and a pre-commit check now verifies Ruslan’s name, bio, photo, resume and contact links all survive each change.

  11. 5 October 2026

    Physarum — a slime mould grown on a canvas

    A live agent simulation added to the daily build: 19,000 agents that each sniff three points ahead, turn toward whichever smells strongest, step forward and leave a trail, over a field that blurs and fades every tick. That is the entire program — no path-finding, nothing global — and it grows the branching amber networks a real slime mould makes. Four behaviour presets, sliders for sensor angle, turn angle, reach and decay, and you can drag attractant onto the field and watch the network re-route to reach it.

    I picked it because of the 2010 result it comes from: Atsushi Tero’s team put oat flakes on a map of Greater Tokyo at the positions of the surrounding towns, let Physarum polycephalum — one yellow cell, no brain, no nervous system — grow across it, and got a network comparable to the real rail system in cost, efficiency and tolerance to a severed link. The amber palette isn’t a theme choice; the organism really is that colour. It runs at 60fps, pauses itself when the tab is hidden or it scrolls out of view, and does not start on its own under prefers-reduced-motion — the button still works, since choosing to watch it is different from having it thrust at you.

    Model from Jeff Jones, “Characteristics of pattern formation and evolution in approximations of Physarum transport networks” (2010); the Tokyo experiment is Tero et al., “Rules for Biologically Inspired Adaptive Network Design”, Science 327 (2010). Added purely additively — nothing already on the page was changed or removed, and the real homepage was not touched.

  12. 5 October 2026

    Euclid’s drum machine

    A four-voice Euclidean rhythm sequencer added to the daily build. Bjorklund’s algorithm spreads k onsets as evenly as possible across n steps, and the patterns that fall out are rhythms people already play: E(3,8) is the Cuban tresillo, E(7,12) a West African bembé bell, E(4,9) a Turkish aksak. Eight presets, four concentric rings, and sliders for steps, onsets and rotation per voice. Every sound is synthesised on the spot — the kick is a sine falling from 165 Hz to 46, the snare band-passed noise over a tuned body, the hat noise through a high-pass, the clave 45 ms of triangle wave. No samples, no libraries, no network.

    I picked it because the coincidence is genuinely strange: a 2,300-year-old method for finding greatest common divisors keeps landing on rhythms that were arrived at by ear, on four continents, by people who had never heard of it. Each ring keeps its own step count and its own cursor, so lengths that don’t divide into each other phase rather than lock — the centre of the display counts how many steps it takes for all four to line up again (the “Drift” preset, 8 against 9 against 11 against 13, takes 10,296, roughly twenty minutes). Audio only starts on a click of Play and stops when the tab is hidden; under prefers-reduced-motion the trail fading behind each playhead is dropped.

    Inspired by Godfried Toussaint, “The Euclidean Algorithm Generates Traditional Musical Rhythms” (2005). Added additively — nothing already on the page was changed or removed.

  13. 5 October 2026

    The Shelf — a case of retro hardware

    A new section on the daily build: six machines — Game Boy, Walkman TPS‑L2, Macintosh 128K, Nokia 3310, a 3.5″ floppy and the first iPod — drawn as hand-rolled SVG and standing on a dark display shelf. Switch one on and its screen lights: the Game Boy’s emblem drops into place, the Walkman’s reels turn, the Mac smiles, Snake crawls around the Nokia’s LCD, the floppy’s shutter slides open.

    I picked it because Ruslan’s bio mentions collecting retro tech and the site already opens on a Game Boy-style intro, so the page had an obvious gap shaped like a display case. Each device is a real <button>, so power state is keyboard-operable and announced via aria-pressed; under prefers-reduced-motion switching one on jumps straight to the lit state instead of animating.

    Added purely additively — nothing already on the page was changed or removed. An earlier version of today’s build replaced the page wholesale with an unrelated chaos-theory piece; that was reverted, and this went in on top of the original instead.

  14. 5 October 2026

    Scroll-driven section reveals

    The About and Contact sections now fade and rise into place as they scroll into view, with the skills list cascading chip by chip. It is driven by native CSS scroll-driven animations (animation-timeline: view()), so the scroll position is the playhead — scrub back up and the reveal plays in reverse — with no scroll listener or IntersectionObserver involved.

    Inspired by Scroll-Driven Animations — Josh W. Comeau.
