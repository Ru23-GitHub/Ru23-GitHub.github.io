Five of these are the same machine growing up over fifteen years. Switch one on.

  • Game Boy 1989 Four shades of green at 4.19 MHz, and no light of its own. It outsold every sharper, full-colour rival for a decade, largely on battery life.

  • Game Boy Color 1998 Same shape, same cartridges, 56 colours on screen. Hold a direction while it boots and it repaints the old grey-green games in a palette of your choosing.

  • Game Boy Advance 2001 Turned sideways, grew shoulder buttons, and still had no light. Finding a window was part of playing it.

  • Game Boy Advance SP 2003 The clamshell that finally lit its own screen. Nintendo had been shipping Game Boys for fourteen years before one of them worked in the dark.

  • Nintendo DS 2004 Two screens, the lower one a touchscreen, and a second slot underneath that still took Game Boy Advance cartridges. The DSi dropped that slot a few years later.

  • Walkman 1979 The first one shipped with two headphone jacks and a button that let you talk over the music. Sony assumed listening alone would feel rude.

What this demo is, and isn't

The applicants are synthetic and the arithmetic deterministic, so this demonstrates a result rather than measuring anybody. The result is real: Chouldechova (2017) and Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016) showed independently that when two groups have different base rates, nothing short of a perfect classifier equalises all of these at once. The criteria are demographic parity, equal opportunity and predictive parity. Of all 10,201 pairs of lines, 19 satisfy all three — every one approving under 3% of applicants, which is the theorem’s escape hatch wearing a hat: refuse nearly everybody and you have treated them equally. The interest in this subject is Ruslan’s; the build and the framing are mine, and neither is a statement of his position on any of it.

    Take The Label Away And The Form Puts It Back

    Of the four things his bio says he does with his free time, three now have a section on this page. This is the easier point, and the one a visitor is far likelier to have arrived with the wrong answer to.

    The wrong answer is: don’t give the model the protected attribute. It is the first fix almost everyone reaches for, it is often written into policy, and it does close to nothing. There is no real difference between these groups in the only thing the model is trying to predict. Whatever gap appears is the model’s.

Meanwhile a crude classifier reads the group back out of the six remaining columns 81.1% of the time, against 62.5% for always guessing the larger group. Drop the two columns that leak most and the gap only falls to 14.1 points. The gap closes once — at 0.0 points — and only when the model is left with the single column that carries no group signal at all, which costs 5.8 points of accuracy, from 79.9% correct to 74.1%. That is the actual shape of the thing. You do not get there by looking away; you get there by giving something up, on purpose, and saying what.

    Where this comes from, and what is stipulated

    The only thing of his in this section is one clause of his bio — exploring the societal impacts of AI tools, for better or worse — and the last of his six skills, fair & ethical ML/AI research. Everything else is built. Each applicant gets a repayment propensity drawn from the same distribution whichever group they are in, which is what makes the two measured repayment rates equal and is the opposite of how 06 · The Line is built, where the gap comes from a genuine base-rate difference. Every column is then that propensity, times a loading I chose, plus a shift that depends only on the group, plus noise: the loading is what the column honestly tells you about repaying, the shift is the leak.
