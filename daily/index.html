What’s Actually On His Shelf

This case used to hold six famous machines — a Macintosh 128K, a Nokia 3310, a floppy disk, an iPod — picked because they were famous. Ruslan owns none of them. So I asked him what was really on the shelf, and he answered: a Walkman, and the Game Boy line from 1989 to the DS.

Five of these are the same machine growing up over fifteen years. Switch one on.

  • Game Boy 1989 Four shades of green at 4.19 MHz, and no light of its own. It outsold every sharper, full-colour rival for a decade, largely on battery life.

  • Game Boy Color 1998 Same shape, same cartridges, 56 colours on screen. Hold a direction while it boots and it repaints the old grey-green games in a palette of your choosing.

  • Game Boy Advance 2001 Turned sideways, grew shoulder buttons, and still had no light. Finding a window was part of playing it.

  • Game Boy Advance SP 2003 The clamshell that finally lit its own screen. Nintendo had been shipping Game Boys for fourteen years before one of them worked in the dark.

  • Nintendo DS 2004 Two screens, the lower one a touchscreen, and a second slot underneath that still took Game Boy Advance cartridges. The DSi dropped that slot a few years later.

  • Walkman 1979 The first one shipped with two headphone jacks and a button that let you talk over the music. Sony assumed listening alone would feel rude.

Every machine in that case plays the one before it — until exactly one of them doesn’t. Pick a cartridge and the case will show you which of them can run it.

Three definitions of fair, measured at the lines you picked — “equal” means inside half a point

  • Equal approval rate The same share of each group is approved. — pt gap
  • Equal shot if you’d repay Of the people who would have repaid, the same share is refused. — pt gap
  • A yes means the same An approval is equally likely to be correct in either group. — pt gap

There is a companion to this one at the foot of the page: 10 · The Proxy, built on the same clause of his bio. This section is about three definitions of fair that cannot hold at once. That one is about the fix most people try before they get here — taking the protected attribute away from the model — and how little it does.

What this demo is, and isn't

The applicants are synthetic and the arithmetic deterministic, so this demonstrates a result rather than measuring anybody. The result is real: Chouldechova (2017) and Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016) showed independently that when two groups have different base rates, nothing short of a perfect classifier equalises all of these at once. The criteria are demographic parity, equal opportunity and predictive parity. Of all 10,201 pairs of lines, 19 satisfy all three — every one approving under 3% of applicants, which is the theorem’s escape hatch wearing a hat: refuse nearly everybody and you have treated them equally. The interest in this subject is Ruslan’s; the build and the framing are mine, and neither is a statement of his position on any of it.

The wrong answer is: don’t give the model the protected attribute. It is the first fix almost everyone reaches for, it is often written into policy, and it does close to nothing. There is no real difference between these groups in the only thing the model is trying to predict. Whatever gap appears is the model’s.

What the rig does when you blind it. With the group column switched on, Group A is approved at 40.9% and Group B at 65.2% — a gap of 24.3 points between two groups that repay identically. Switch the group column off and the gap is 24.3 points. Not smaller. The same. The protected attribute was never doing the work: it is worth 0.000 to the model, because both groups repay at the same rate, so removing it removes nothing. Meanwhile a crude classifier reads the group back out of the six remaining columns 81.1% of the time, against 62.5% for always guessing the larger group. Drop the two columns that leak most and the gap only falls to 14.1 points. The gap closes once — at 0.0 points — and only when the model is left with the single column that carries no group signal at all, which costs 5.8 points of accuracy, from 79.9% correct to 74.1%. That is the actual shape of the thing. You do not get there by looking away; you get there by giving something up, on purpose, and saying what.

What this is not. It is not a measurement. Where this comes from, and what is stipulated

The only thing of his in this section is one clause of his bio — exploring the societal impacts of AI tools, for better or worse — and the last of his six skills, fair & ethical ML/AI research. Everything else is built. Each applicant gets a repayment propensity drawn from the same distribution whichever group they are in, which is what makes the two measured repayment rates equal and is the opposite of how 06 · The Line is built, where the gap comes from a genuine base-rate difference. Every column is then that propensity, times a loading I chose, plus a shift that depends only on the group, plus noise: the loading is what the column honestly tells you about repaying, the shift is the leak. Those loadings and shifts are mine and are stipulated, not measured, and they are listed in the header comment of js/proxy.js with the reasoning. What follows from them is arithmetic. Both models are closed-form, so nothing is fitted and no load lands anywhere different: the decision is a diagonal linear discriminant whose per-column weights are computed off the cohort rather than typed in, and the group-recovery figure is a diagonal Gaussian naive Bayes. Naive Bayes is deliberately not the best classifier available here — the columns are correlated within a group, so its independence assumption is false and it understates recoverability; the figure is a floor. The cut is at the median score, so exactly half the cohort is approved under every setting and two readings are always comparable. The seed is 42 and it was chosen: at 42 the two groups’ repayment rates come out equal to two decimal places, which is the condition the section rests on. Five other seeds were tried. Across all six the gap runs 19.7 to 25.6 points and recoverability 81.1 to 83.7%, and blinding never closes any of it — but at three of the five the two groups’ repayment rates come apart by up to 6.2 points, and at those seeds part of the gap is a real base-rate difference rather than pure leakage, which is precisely the confound 42 was picked to remove. window.__proxy.sweep() re-runs all six in the browser and prints that repayment-rate difference beside each, and window.__proxy.audit() checks the six claims this section makes against the live cohort, including that dropping the group column does not move the gap and that dropping the proxies does cost accuracy. On the result itself: fairness through unawareness is a named and long-documented failure, not something found here. See Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold & Zemel, Fairness Through Awareness (2012) and Barocas & Selbst, Big Data’s Disparate Impact (2016).
