Things That Booted

Ruslan collects retro tech, so here is a case of it. Six machines that taught a lot of people what a computer was supposed to feel like — switch one on.

  • Game Boy 1989 Four shades of green at 4.19 MHz. It outsold every sharper, full-colour rival for a decade, largely on battery life.

  • Walkman TPS‑L2 1979 The first one shipped with two headphone jacks and a button that let you talk over the music. Sony assumed listening alone would feel rude.

  • Macintosh 128K 1984 128 KB of RAM, no hard disk, and the first mouse most people had ever put a hand on.

  • Nokia 3310 2000 133 grams, a week between charges, and Snake II. Around 126 million of them left the factory.

  • 3.5″ Floppy Disk 1983 A rigid shell and a spring-loaded shutter over 1.44 MB. It has now spent longer as the save icon than it ever spent as storage.

  • iPod 2001 Five gigabytes, a thousand songs, and a scroll wheel that physically spun under your thumb. The next generation replaced it with a touch ring.

Every device above is hand-drawn SVG — no images, no libraries.

Somebody Picks The Line

Ruslan’s bio says he is interested in exploring the societal impacts of AI tools, for better or worse, and his skills list ends with fair & ethical ML/AI research. Until today that was the only trace of it on this page — one clause of text, sitting above a case of retro gadgets.

So: below, a model has scored 1,591 loan applicants on how likely each is to repay. It is a reasonable model, and it is a little less sure about Group B, because it saw a third as many of them. Somebody still has to pick the score at which the answer becomes yes, and that part was never a modelling problem.

Move the lines, then read the three definitions of “fair” underneath. Any one of them you can satisfy. All three at once is possible too — there is a button — and the page will show you the bill.

Three definitions of fair, measured at the lines you picked — “equal” means inside half a point

  • Equal approval rate The same share of each group is approved. — pt gap
  • Equal shot if you’d repay Of the people who would have repaid, the same share is refused. — pt gap
  • A yes means the same An approval is equally likely to be correct in either group. — pt gap

The applicants are synthetic and the arithmetic is deterministic — two normal densities per group, rounded to whole people — so this demonstrates a result rather than measuring anybody. The result is real: Chouldechova (2017) and Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016) showed independently that when two groups have different base rates, nothing short of a perfect classifier equalises all of these at once. The criteria here are demographic parity, equal opportunity and predictive parity. A scan of all 10,201 pairs of lines found 19 that do satisfy all three — every one of them approving under 3% of applicants, which is the theorem’s “perfect classifier” escape hatch wearing a different hat: refuse nearly everybody and you have treated them equally. Above a 5% approval rate, zero of 3,306 pairs qualify. The interest in this subject is Ruslan’s; the build and the framing are the agent’s, and neither is a statement of his position on any of it.
