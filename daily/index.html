The Butterfly
Effect

In the winter of 1961 a rounding error of one part in a thousand wrecked a weather forecast, and chaos theory fell out of the wreckage. Here is an attractor you can turn, and two machines for feeling why.

↻ Drag the shape to turn it

0 — the rounding error

Lorenz types four digits instead of seven

Edward Lorenz was a meteorologist at MIT with a Royal McBee LGP-30 — a desk-sized vacuum-tube computer that chewed through a toy twelve-variable weather model at roughly one simulated day per minute. One winter morning in 1961 he wanted a longer look at a run he’d already done, so rather than start over he restarted it from the middle, typing the numbers back in off the printout.

The printout had been rounded to three decimal places. The machine had been holding six. Where Lorenz typed 0.506, the computer had been carrying 0.506127 — a discrepancy of about one part in five thousand, far smaller than any instrument could ever measure the real atmosphere to.

He went for coffee. When he came back the new run had, within a couple of simulated months, nothing whatsoever to do with the old one. Not drifted. Unrecognisable. His first assumption was a blown vacuum tube.

The seagull later became a butterfly, and not by Lorenz’s hand: when he gave a talk to the AAAS in 1972 he hadn’t supplied a title, so the session convener Philip Merilees invented one for him — “Predictability: Does the Flap of a Butterfly’s Wings in Brazil Set Off a Tornado in Texas?” The name stuck to the whole field.

The shape turning at the top of this page is the three-equation sketch Lorenz boiled his model down to in 1963. It isn’t weather; it’s a cartoon of hot fluid rolling over in a pan. But it does the one thing weather does that broke his forecast, and it does it where you can watch.

The straight stretch is the whole story: the gap doesn’t creep, it multiplies, roughly doubling every 0.76 time units no matter how small you start it. Then it flattens — not because the worlds re-converge, but because they’re both stuck on the same finite shape and you simply cannot be more than one attractor apart. Every extra decimal place you measure the atmosphere to buys you the same small, fixed amount of extra warning. That is the ceiling, and it is why there is no ten-week forecast.

2 — the smallest chaotic thing

One line of arithmetic, run forever

You don’t need three coupled differential equations. Take a number between 0 and 1, and repeat x ← r x (1 − x) — a population that grows in proportion to how many there are and how much room is left. Then nudge r upward and watch the thing lose its mind.

3.200

The splits come faster and faster — r ≈ 3, then 3.449, then 3.544 — piling up at 3.56995, past which the orbit never repeats at all. The ratio of those gaps converges on 4.6692…, Feigenbaum’s constant, and it is the same number for a huge family of unrelated equations: dripping taps, buckling beams, oscillating circuits. Slide out to 3.83 and chaos abruptly gives way to a clean three-cycle, then shatters again. Robert May put this in Nature in 1976 partly as a plea to teach it in schools, on the grounds that an intuition built on tidy equations is an actively misleading one.

3 — so

Determinism was never the same thing as predictability

Nothing on this page is random. Every pixel came from arithmetic a pocket calculator could do, started from numbers I chose, and would come out identical if you ran it again. And still: knowing the rules exactly, and the starting state almost exactly, buys you an horizon measured in days rather than forever.

Lorenz’s paper sat mostly unread in a meteorology journal for roughly a decade before the rest of science noticed it. Which is its own kind of sensitive dependence.
