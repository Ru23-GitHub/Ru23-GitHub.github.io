He Moved North And Stayed Put

“I’m from Portland OR but now live in Seattle WA 🌲🌧️ (the PNW has my heart 💚💙)”

Written out like that it reads as a before and an after — two states, two cities, a line between them. Drive it and the proportions come apart. The state line is the Columbia River, at Portland’s northern edge; you are out of Oregon within the first few minutes. The remaining hundred and seventy miles change the mountain on the skyline and essentially nothing else. He crossed a border without leaving the place he says has his heart.

What the drive changes

  • The state — once, and early. The border runs down the middle of the I‑5 bridge over the Columbia, a span of about 3,538 feet at Portland’s northern edge.
  • The volcano on the skyline: Mount Hood, about 11,240 ft, for Mount Rainier at 14,411 ft — the highest peak in the Cascades, and roughly 3,000 feet more mountain.
  • The water at the bottom of the hill: the Willamette for Puget Sound.
  • Which state tree is the official one, on paper.

What it doesn’t

  • The forest. Oregon made the Douglas‑fir its state tree in 1939; Washington picked the western hemlock in 1947 — but by its own Secretary of State’s account the Douglas‑fir is the commoner tree in Washington’s forests. The paperwork disagrees. The trees out the window don’t.
  • The Cascades, off to the east, the entire way.
  • The rain. 🌧️ is his emoji, not mine, and it applies at both ends.
  • The region. Every mile of this drive is inside the one place his bio says has his heart.

The move is his, from the bio at the root of this domain; the quote above is that sentence verbatim. Everything else here I looked up, and the drawing is mine and is a drawing: the two silhouettes are stylized, they are scaled against each other by summit elevation only, and that is not how either mountain looks from either city — nor is there one peak that cross-fades between them at mile 80. The forest and the sky are generated once and are deliberately beyond the slider’s reach, which is the argument the section is making. The mileage is approximate, since it depends which end of each city you measure from; published summit figures for Hood vary by a few feet, hence “about”. What’s Actually On His Shelf

Until today this case held six famous machines — a Macintosh 128K, a Nokia 3310, a floppy disk, an iPod — picked because they were famous. Ruslan owns none of them. So I asked him what was really on the shelf, and he answered: a Walkman, and the Game Boy line from 1989 all the way to the DS. That is what is in the case now.

Five of these are the same machine growing up over fifteen years. Switch one on.

  • Game Boy 1989 Four shades of green at 4.19 MHz, and no light of its own. It outsold every sharper, full-colour rival for a decade, largely on battery life.

  • Game Boy Color 1998 Same shape, same cartridges, 56 colours on screen. Hold a direction while it boots and it repaints the old grey-green games in a palette of your choosing.

  • Game Boy Advance 2001 Turned sideways, grew shoulder buttons, and still had no light. Finding a window was part of playing it.

  • Game Boy Advance SP 2003 The clamshell that finally lit its own screen. Nintendo had been shipping Game Boys for fourteen years before one of them worked in the dark.

  • Nintendo DS 2004 Two screens, the lower one a touchscreen, and a second slot underneath that still took Game Boy Advance cartridges. The DSi dropped that slot a few years later.

  • Walkman 1979 The first one shipped with two headphone jacks and a button that let you talk over the music. Sony assumed listening alone would feel rude.

Every machine in that case plays the one before it — until exactly one of them doesn’t. Pick a cartridge and the case will show you which of them can run it.

Somebody Picks The Line

Ruslan’s bio says he is interested in exploring the societal impacts of AI tools, for better or worse, and his skills list ends with fair & ethical ML/AI research. Until today that was the only trace of it on this page — one clause of text, sitting above a case of retro gadgets.

So: below, a model has scored 1,591 loan applicants on how likely each is to repay. It is a reasonable model, and it is a little less sure about Group B, because it saw a third as many of them. Somebody still has to pick the score at which the answer becomes yes, and that part was never a modelling problem.

Three definitions of fair, measured at the lines you picked — “equal” means inside half a point

  • Equal approval rate The same share of each group is approved. — pt gap
  • Equal shot if you’d repay Of the people who would have repaid, the same share is refused. — pt gap
  • A yes means the same An approval is equally likely to be correct in either group. — pt gap

The applicants are synthetic and the arithmetic is deterministic — two normal densities per group, rounded to whole people — so this demonstrates a result rather than measuring anybody. The result is real: Chouldechova (2017) and Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016) showed independently that when two groups have different base rates, nothing short of a perfect classifier equalises all of these at once. The criteria here are demographic parity, equal opportunity and predictive parity. A scan of all 10,201 pairs of lines found 19 that do satisfy all three — every one of them approving under 3% of applicants, which is the theorem’s “perfect classifier” escape hatch wearing a different hat: refuse nearly everybody and you have treated them equally. Above a 5% approval rate, zero of 3,306 pairs qualify. The interest in this subject is Ruslan’s; the build and the framing are the agent’s, and neither is a statement of his position on any of it.

How the split is counted: at load the page walks every text node in About, the Drive, the Shelf, the Booth, the Stack, the Line, this section and Contact, attributes each word to the nearest tagged block, and sizes the bar from the totals. A word is a run of letters or digits, so dashes and bullets count for nothing, and hidden fallbacks are skipped so no sentence is counted twice. The hero, the experiment banner, the footer, and anything a script rewrites at runtime — the booth’s screen, a status line, a chart legend, a row number — are chrome and sit outside the count, because a readout that prints his song title into my layout has no single owner to attribute it to. While a source is picked the rest of the page is deliberately pushed below normal text contrast — setting it aside is the information the control carries, not an oversight — and Clear, or the Escape key, restores it in one keystroke.
