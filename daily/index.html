The Shelf

Things That Booted

Ruslan collects retro tech, so here is a case of it. Six machines that taught a lot of people what a computer was supposed to feel like — switch one on.

  • Game Boy 1989 Four shades of green at 4.19 MHz. It outsold every sharper, full-colour rival for a decade, largely on battery life.

  • Walkman TPS‑L2 1979 The first one shipped with two headphone jacks and a button that let you talk over the music. Sony assumed listening alone would feel rude.

  • Macintosh 128K 1984 128 KB of RAM, no hard disk, and the first mouse most people had ever put a hand on.

  • Nokia 3310 2000 133 grams, a week between charges, and Snake II. Around 126 million of them left the factory.

  • 3.5″ Floppy Disk 1983 A rigid shell and a spring-loaded shutter over 1.44 MB. It has now spent longer as the save icon than it ever spent as storage.

  • iPod 2001 Five gigabytes, a thousand songs, and a scroll wheel that physically spun under your thumb. The next generation replaced it with a touch ring.

Every device above is hand-drawn SVG — no images, no libraries.

Euclid

A Drum Machine Made Of Long Division

Ask for k beats spread as evenly as possible across n slots and the answer falls out of Euclid’s 2,300-year-old algorithm for the greatest common divisor. The strange part is what comes back. E(3,8) is the Cuban tresillo. E(7,12) is a West African bembé bell. E(4,9) is a Turkish aksak. Nobody designed these from the maths — the maths just keeps landing on rhythms people were already playing. Four rings below, one per voice. Each keeps its own step count, so an eight against a nine drifts instead of locking.

98 bpm 70%
  1. Kick E(3,8)
  2. Snare E(2,8)
  3. Hat E(5,8)
  4. Clave E(3,8)

Nothing here is a recording. The kick is a sine wave falling from 165 Hz to 46, the snare is band-passed noise over a short tuned body, the hat is noise through a high-pass, the clave is 45 milliseconds of triangle wave. Sound starts only when you press Play, and stops if you switch tabs. Pattern generation is Bjorklund’s algorithm, from Godfried Toussaint’s paper “The Euclidean Algorithm Generates Traditional Musical Rhythms” (2005).

This one needs JavaScript and the Web Audio API — without them the console above is just a diagram.

Try Drift, then set every ring to a different prime.
